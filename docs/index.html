Predicting Future Content Performance Decline

A supervised machine-learning study using historical search, engagement, demand, and content signals to predict future content performance trajectories.

Abstract

This study asks whether historical search, engagement, and content signals can predict which content pages are likely to experience meaningful future performance changes. A temporal supervised-learning design was applied to anonymized FlyRank content-performance data, using the previous three calendar months as historical context and the following month as the prediction period. Random Forest and XGBoost models were compared with a most-frequent baseline using a time-based train/test split. The Random Forest achieved 58.57% accuracy and 54.29% balanced accuracy, while XGBoost achieved 62.93% accuracy and 50.55% balanced accuracy. Recent page-level performance was the strongest predictive signal, supporting the use of historical behavior as a prioritization signal while not establishing any causal relationship.

1. Introduction

Content performance changes over time. Some pages continue to grow, some remain relatively stable, and others experience meaningful declines. Identifying pages that may require attention can therefore be useful for content and SEO decision-making.

Traditional monitoring approaches often focus on individual performance metrics such as impressions, clicks, or sessions. This study investigates whether combining historical performance, engagement, search-demand signals, and content characteristics can provide a more useful prediction of future performance trajectory.

Research Question: Can historical search, engagement, and content signals predict which content pages are likely to experience meaningful future performance decline?

The analysis is framed as a predictive decision-support problem. Predictions are intended to help prioritize pages for human review and do not establish that a particular content intervention causes future performance changes.

2. Methodology

2.1 Research Design

The study uses supervised machine learning with a temporal prediction design. Historical information available before a prediction month is used to construct predictors, while the subsequent month is used only to define the future outcome.

2.2 Prediction Unit

Each observation represents a content page for a client at a defined prediction month. Page and client identifiers are anonymized and are used only to preserve page-level and client-level boundaries during analysis.

2.3 Prediction Window

For each prediction month, the previous three calendar months form the historical reference period. The following prediction month is treated as the future outcome period.

2.4 Target Definition

Performance change was calculated by comparing impressions in the prediction month with the average monthly impressions across the previous three calendar months.

Pages required at least 30 impressions across the previous three months to receive a target label.

Class Definition
Declining Performance change ≤ −20%
Stable −20% < performance change < +20%
Growing Performance change ≥ +20%

2.5 Features

The modeling dataset combines historical impressions, clicks, sessions, engaged sessions, engagement time, AI sessions, search-demand signals, backlinks, keyword characteristics, URL characteristics, and content size measures.

Snapshot publication/deletion fields were excluded from modeling.

2.6 Temporal Validation

A time-based split was used to reduce temporal leakage and better represent future prediction.

4. Limitations

  1. Predictive, not causal: The models identify statistical patterns but do not establish that any feature or intervention causes performance changes.
  2. Temporal metadata limitation: Some content metadata comes from warehouse-level snapshots rather than fully time-versioned historical records.
  3. Class imbalance and distribution shift: Target-class proportions differ between training and testing periods.
  4. Stable-class difficulty: Stable observations were more difficult for the model to classify reliably.
  5. Anonymized data: The public artifact cannot identify specific websites, clients, or content pages.
  6. Finite observation period: The dataset covers a limited historical period and should be re-evaluated on newer data.
  7. Decision-support scope: Predictions should support human review rather than automatically determine content actions.

5. Ranked Recommendations

1 Prioritize pages showing recent performance deterioration

Recent performance was the strongest predictive signal, so pages showing sustained deterioration should receive the highest review priority.

2 Compare recent impressions with historical performance

Recent impressions and three-month averages can help distinguish temporary variation from a meaningful trajectory change.

3 Investigate clicks and sessions

Declining impressions accompanied by declining clicks or sessions provide additional evidence for human review.

4 Review content depth and structure

Content size variables contributed to prediction and can provide useful context when reviewing at-risk pages.

5 Use demand and authority signals as supporting evidence

Search volume, backlinks, keyword characteristics, and category structure should support rather than replace performance analysis.

6 Keep a human in the decision loop

Model predictions should be used to prioritize review, not treated as guaranteed diagnoses or automated intervention decisions.

Recommended workflow: Detect deterioration → Compare historical baseline → Check engagement → Review content → Consider demand/authority → Human decision

6. Reproducibility

The analysis uses the publicly provided FlyRank/internship-warehouse dataset hosted on Hugging Face.

The analysis uses anonymized daily content-performance data and content metadata. Feature construction, target labeling, temporal splitting, model training, evaluation, and visualization are contained in the accompanying capstone notebook.

The Random Forest and XGBoost experiments use a fixed random seed of 42.

The modeling workflow uses a temporal train/test split rather than a random split. No credentials, private queries, client names, identifying URLs, or raw private exports are included in the public artifact.
